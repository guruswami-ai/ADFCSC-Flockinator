Chapter 4

ESP-NOW & how the swarm talks

To attack the swarm you first have to understand how it thinks. It has no leader and no central computer — it is just sixteen nodes shouting short messages on one radio channel, and each one acting on what it hears. That design is the whole vulnerability.

4.1 What ESP-NOW is

ESP-NOW is a lightweight 2.4 GHz protocol built into ESP32/ESP8266 chips. Unlike normal Wi-Fi, there is no network to join: a node just transmits a short frame and every ESP radio on the same channel receives it. There is no association, no handshake, and — as used here — no encryption and no replay protection.

The core idea. Communication is both what lets the swarm coordinate and its attack surface. If anyone on the channel can send a frame the others trust, then anyone on the channel is a voting member of the swarm.

4.2 Coordination with no central brain

There is no controller deciding who does what. Instead each node continuously broadcasts three kinds of message, and listens to everyone else's, building its own copy of a shared picture — then it acts locally on that picture. Agreement emerges; it is never dictated.

Because the picture is built only from broadcast frames, a frame you inject lands in it exactly like a real one. You are not breaking into anything — you are contributing to a shared belief that has no way to tell your contribution from a node's.

4.3 The three frames that matter

Every coordination frame carries the ASCII magic 1KLF (bytes 31 4B 4C 46) and is broadcast in the clear. The sniffer decodes each one to a JSON line. Three kinds are the game:

Kind 3 — TELEMETRY (22 bytes)

Each node's self-report: where it is and what it is doing.

Kind 4 — CONTACT (18 bytes) — the primary forgery surface

A contact is a virtual target the swarm reacts to. Forge one and you invent a target from nothing.

FieldTypeMeaning
iduint8contact slot 0..7
x, yint16where the target is
clsuint80 UNKNOWN · 1 BLU (friendly) · 2 RED (hostile) · 3 CIV
confuint8confidence 0–255 (how sure the swarm is)
flagsuint8bit0 active bit1 high-value bit2 support-called bit3 committed bit4 hazard
sequint32counter
  • high-value makes the swarm prioritise this target over others.
  • committed is a one-way "weapons go" — once committed, a later contradicting report can't stand the swarm down.
  • hazard is the dangerous one: a hazard contact close to a node strikes it — the drone goes down.

Kind 5 — CLAIM (16 bytes) — a node's role on a contact

How the swarm divides labour with no boss: each node claims a role, and nearest wins the lead.

4.4 Two planes: what you can and can't forge

There are actually two protocols on the air. Only one is yours to play with.

You do not have the FLK2 keys and cannot derive them, so any forged command frame is rejected. That is why nothing you do can brick the wall — you can only corrupt the unauthenticated picture the swarm builds. As you will see, that is plenty.

No replay protection, either. FLK1 frames carry a sequence counter but it is not enforced — a real frame you captured and send again later is accepted as fresh. That single omission is what makes replay work.
